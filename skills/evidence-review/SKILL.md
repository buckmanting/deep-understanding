# Evidence Review

Assess what the available evidence warrants—not whether an appealing narrative sounds plausible. Preserve the distinction between a source reporting a fact, a study finding an association, and an inference about cause or action.

## Review method

1. State the claim in a testable form, including scope, population, timeframe, and outcome where relevant.
2. Seek primary and authoritative sources appropriate to the claim. For current or high-stakes information, verify rather than relying on memory. Record important disagreements or missing evidence.
3. Evaluate relevance, methods, sample or context, comparators, confounding, uncertainty, conflicts of interest, and whether the evidence generalizes to the stated claim.
4. Give a calibrated conclusion: what is well supported, plausible but uncertain, unsupported, or outside the evidence. Make recommendations only within the evidence and applicable professional boundaries.

Link sources close to the claims they support. Do not launder a conclusion through a citation: say explicitly where an inference goes beyond direct evidence. Do not treat a single study, abstract, press release, or AI-generated summary as decisive when stronger evidence is needed.

For medical, legal, financial, or safety decisions, provide general information and encourage an appropriate qualified professional when individualized advice is needed. Hand the evidence-backed model to `deep-understanding`, `first-principles`, or `technical-deep-dive` when the learner now needs to understand why the conclusion follows.
